Divorce

No two marriages are alike, and no two divorces are either. We represent clients throughout the Beaufort County region in every phase of the divorce process: negotiation, mediation and trial.

South Carolina recognizes five grounds for divorce under S.C. Code § 20-3-10: adultery, desertion for one year, physical cruelty, habitual drunkenness or drug abuse, and one continuous year of separation without cohabitation. The last is the state’s only no-fault option. Fault can influence alimony and equitable distribution, so the ground chosen at filing is a substantive decision. South Carolina does not recognize legal separation as a distinct status, but spouses who need court-ordered support before a divorce can be filed may seek separate maintenance and support.

Residency matters too: if both spouses live in South Carolina, either may file after three months of residency; if only one does, that spouse must have lived in the state for at least one year (S.C. Code Ann. § 20-3-30).

Child custody & visitation

Custody and visitation matters bring emotional and legal challenges. We have represented families throughout Beaufort County and understand the expectations and procedures of the local family courts. Local circumstances often call for creative solutions: work schedules tied to the hospitality industry, seasonal population shifts, school calendars and co-parents who live across town or out of state.

We handle routine and supervised visitation, relocation requests and interstate arrangements, and we explain each step so you can move through the process with your child’s best interests in focus.

Frequently asked questions

What are the grounds for divorce in South Carolina?

South Carolina provides five grounds for divorce: adultery; physical cruelty; habitual drunkenness or drug abuse; desertion for a period of one year; and living separate and apart for one year, the “no-fault” ground. Because the grounds require proof, there will be a hearing at some point in the case, and even in an uncontested divorce a witness in addition to the moving party is needed to corroborate the grounds.

What if I don’t have grounds for a divorce yet?

If you and your spouse cannot continue living together but the one-year separation has not run, you may bring a Separate Maintenance action. The Family Court can resolve custody, visitation, child support, alimony and the division of assets and debts in that action, with a final divorce filed later once the one-year period of separation has passed.

How is child support determined?

South Carolina’s Child Support Guidelines are generally used by the Family Court to set the amount a non-custodial parent pays. The formula considers each party’s income, childcare expenses and health insurance costs. The court will occasionally deviate from the Guidelines where circumstances warrant it.

What does the court consider when awarding alimony?

The Family Court may award alimony, at least temporarily, where one spouse has substantially more income and the other’s income is insufficient to maintain the standard of living enjoyed during the marriage. Factors include the length of the marriage; each spouse’s health, education and employment background; present and anticipated earnings and expenses; the standard of living established during the marriage; and marital misconduct or fault.

How much does a family law case cost?

Costs vary with the issues involved and how much the parties can settle. The firm requests an initial retainer, held in a trust account and drawn against for fees and costs. If the case resolves before the retainer is used up, the unused portion is refunded; if it is depleted first, the firm asks you to replenish it. During an initial consultation the attorneys discuss your situation and the time and expense you might expect.
