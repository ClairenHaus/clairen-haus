Estate Planning

A good estate plan says who receives what, who makes decisions if you can’t, and how to protect the people who depend on you, including family members with disabilities.

The core documents

Most plans start with a will, which names who receives your property and who serves as executor, and can name a guardian for minor children. A trust can hold property for beneficiaries over time and can keep some assets out of probate. A durable power of attorney lets someone you choose handle finances if you can’t, and health care documents name who can make medical decisions for you and record your wishes.

Special needs trusts

A family member with a disability may depend on public benefits that have strict limits on assets. A properly drafted special needs trust can hold money for that person’s benefit to pay for things benefits don’t cover, without leaving it to them outright. The drafting details matter, which is why this work is a large part of Ms. Green’s practice.

Connecticut considerations

Connecticut is one of the few states with its own estate and gift tax, and beneficiary designations on accounts and insurance pass outside a will, so a plan should look at the whole picture. Reviewing a plan after a marriage, divorce, birth, death or move keeps it current.
