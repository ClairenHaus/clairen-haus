What is Chapter 7?

Chapter 7 is part of the bankruptcy law (Chapter 7 of Title 11 of the United States Code). It is sometimes called a “liquidation” because nonexempt assets may be liquidated as part of the case: property that is not exempt can be taken and sold by the Trustee to pay unsecured creditors.

A debtor can usually prevent that by paying cash for the nonexempt property, or by reaching an agreement under which the Trustee accepts an exempt asset of roughly equivalent value. A Chapter 7 case begins with a petition, filed with the bankruptcy court, listing all assets and liabilities and other information.

Bankruptcy exemptions in Florida

Each state’s legislature sets exemptions for people sued in that state, and those same exemptions control what property a debtor who meets the domicile requirements may keep in a Chapter 7 case. Florida’s exemptions are unique to Florida and include important protections for personal property and real estate, including the homestead exemption.

How far Florida’s unlimited homestead exemption can be used depends on how long the homestead has been owned and other legal considerations. It should not be assumed that it applies in every case.

Domicile and residency

A Chapter 7 debtor may use Florida’s exemptions if the filer has lived in Florida for at least two years before filing.

A debtor who doesn’t meet that requirement may still be able to file in Florida if they have lived in Florida longer than in any other state during the previous 180 days. In practice, once a debtor has lived in Florida for 91 days, venue is proper here, but they will need to use another set of exemptions, evaluated case by case.

Questions about bankruptcy

Who can use Florida’s bankruptcy exemptions?

A Chapter 7 debtor may use Florida’s exemptions if the filer has been a resident of Florida for at least two years before filing.

Does Florida’s unlimited homestead exemption apply in every bankruptcy?

No. How far Florida’s homestead exemption can be used depends on how long the homestead has been owned and other legal considerations. Each case has to be evaluated on its own facts.
