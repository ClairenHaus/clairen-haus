Estate planning, trusts & wills

A plan built around your objectives and needs, or a couple’s, which often includes avoiding probate and keeping costs and expenses down after death.

Planning that protects the people you love

Estate planning should be structured around an individual’s objectives and needs or, for a married couple, the couple’s. Often that means avoiding probate and minimizing the costs and expenses that follow a death. It starts with a conversation about your specific situation, goals and wishes, so the plan protects your spouse, family and other intended beneficiaries.

With proper estate planning it is often possible to avoid probate, and the significant attorney’s fees and expenses that typically come with it, altogether.

What Kevin prepares

  • Wills
  • Living, revocable trusts
  • Durable powers of attorney, sometimes with “super” powers
  • Designations of health care surrogate
  • Ladybird deeds (enhanced life estate deeds)
  • Pre-need declarations of guardian
  • Probate and trust administration documents

Living trusts: the signing is only the start

A living trust is one of a number of tools for avoiding probate, but it takes careful planning and drafting. A living trust does not automatically avoid probate: if it is not properly structured and funded, probate may still be required.

Probate avoidance and the proper protection of your family are not achieved just by signing a trust agreement. Post-signing planning, guidance and legal advice are critical.

Own real estate in another state? Even if all of your Florida property is in a trust, a Florida probate proceeding is likely to be required if you own real estate elsewhere. A living trust can be especially important in that situation.

Powers of attorney and future Medicaid planning

Durable powers of attorney can work together with an estate plan, particularly where someone might need to apply for Medicaid at a later date. They are worth discussing in detail when the plan is drafted.

Ladybird deeds and adding someone to title

Ladybird deeds, also known as enhanced life estate deeds, are sometimes used to avoid probate for real estate. Be careful about which type of deed is used when adding another person to title: creditors of the added person might make a claim against the property or try to attach it.

Related planning tools

Designation of health care surrogate. Florida’s statutes were revised effective October 2015 to allow designations that are effective immediately.

Pre-need declaration of guardian. A written declaration naming who you would want to serve as your guardian, should one ever be needed.

Questions about estate planning, trusts and wills

Does a living trust automatically avoid probate?

No. A living, revocable trust is one of several tools for avoiding probate, but it has to be carefully planned, drafted and funded. If a living trust is not properly structured and funded, probate may still be required. Post-signing planning, guidance and legal advice matter as much as the signing itself.

Why does owning real estate in another state matter for my estate plan?

Even if all of your Florida property is held in a trust, a Florida probate proceeding is likely to be required if you own real estate in another state. That is one reason a living trust can be especially important for people who own property in more than one state.

What is a ladybird deed?

A ladybird deed, also known as an enhanced life estate deed, is sometimes used to avoid probate for real estate. Care is needed when adding another person to title, because creditors of the added person might make a claim against the property or try to attach it.

Can a designation of health care surrogate take effect right away?

Yes. Florida’s statutes were revised effective October 2015 to allow health care surrogate designations that are effective immediately.
