Special needs trusts

A special needs trust holds property for a person with a disability. Set up with care, it can provide for that person without undoing the planning around them.

How Attorney Hoyle helps

Protecting and preserving the share of a minor, disabled or elderly beneficiary is one of the things a well-built estate plan can do. Attorney Hoyle prepares trust instruments that include the terms and conditions needed to comply with applicable law and regulations, and helps trustees with the administration that follows.

In plain terms

Two broad types

Self-settled special needs trusts are irrevocable living trusts created for the benefit of a person with a disability and funded with that person’s own property. Third-party special needs trusts are funded with property that belongs to someone else, such as a parent or grandparent.

How a trust is created

Except for a pooled trust, every special needs trust requires the person creating and funding it to sign a written trust instrument. Like any living trust, it must name at least one settlor, one trustee and one beneficiary, have a valid purpose and sufficient terms to permit its administration, and actually own property. In practice, property is transferred into the trust’s name when the instrument is signed.

Why the details matter

The rules for these trusts are technical, and the right type depends on whose money funds the trust and what the beneficiary needs. That is a conversation to have with an attorney before any documents are signed.
