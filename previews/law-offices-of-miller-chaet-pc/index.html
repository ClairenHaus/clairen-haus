Our approach

Honest about what you actually need.

Our philosophy is to leave as little as possible in an estate to be controlled by a will, so we also help clients with beneficiary designations and property titles, so family members are not overburdened with probate administration.

Trusts are a good example. They can smooth out the transfer of out-of-state property, keep an inheritance safe until a beneficiary is ready, and help provide for future generations. They are also expensive to create and manage, so we don’t use them for everybody. We tell you whether a trust is a beneficial technique for your situation.

Common questions

Answers before you call.

What happens if I die without a will in Georgia?

Your assets are distributed according to Georgia’s intestacy laws, which means they go to your closest living relatives as assigned by state law. A will lets you decide who inherits, name an executor, and name a guardian for minor children.

Do I need a trust?

Not always. Trusts are expensive to create and manage, and the firm does not use them for everybody. The attorneys advise whether a trust is a beneficial technique for your situation, for example to avoid a separate probate for property in another state, or to control when beneficiaries receive funds.

Is estate planning only for older people?

No. Anyone can become incapacitated at any age. Financial powers of attorney and a Georgia Advance Directive for Health Care let you choose who makes decisions for you and when that transition takes place.

What is a Special Needs Trust?

A place for you or others to give gifts to a family member with a disability without those gifts counting against eligibility for government benefits such as Medicaid. The trust is meant for needs beyond what benefits cover, such as therapies, speech coaching, entertainment and vacations.
