Trusts

Trusts are like picnic baskets: they tell one person, the trustee, how to hold and manage assets for other people, the beneficiaries.

Living or testamentary

A trust can be testamentary, created after death through a will, or living, created during your lifetime.

Good reasons to create a trust

  • Property in another state. Without a trust, a separate ancillary probate would have to be opened in every state where a person owns property. Putting out-of-state property into a trust avoids the problem.
  • Beneficiaries who are not ready yet. A trust can set ages of withdrawal and limit the occasions on which beneficiaries receive funds.
  • Providing for future generations. Trusts can reduce wealth transfer taxes. The firm sets up marital trusts, disclaimer trusts, life insurance trusts and residence trusts.

Not for everybody

Trusts are expensive to create and manage, and the firm doesn’t use them for everybody. The attorneys advise whether a trust is a beneficial estate planning technique for your specific situation, and set up the trusts they believe will best achieve your goals.

Questions about trusts

Do I need a trust?

Not always. Trusts are expensive to create and manage, and the firm does not use them for everybody. The attorneys advise whether a trust is a beneficial technique for your situation, for example to avoid a separate probate for property in another state, or to control when beneficiaries receive funds.

What kinds of trusts does the firm set up?

Living and testamentary trusts, including marital trusts, disclaimer trusts, life insurance trusts and residence trusts, as well as Special Needs Trusts.
