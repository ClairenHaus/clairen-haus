Estate planning and pre-planning.

Planning ahead protects your estate for your heirs and makes sure your wishes are carried out, both for your health care and for how your property is distributed.

Crossing the bridge before you get to it

Many people moving into their own retirement years find that an aging parent or other family member needs significant attention. Older adults often want to stay independent and self-sufficient even as daily life gets harder. Honest, open conversations, and keeping your loved one involved in decisions, let you become an effective advocate. Learning about their health, doctors, medications, spiritual needs and home now will reward you later.

What estate planning includes

  • Health care planning, such as a living will or naming a health care surrogate
  • A durable power of attorney
  • Tax considerations
  • Life insurance and long-term care insurance needs
  • A will or a living trust

Wills and trusts are the most common ways to pass on property. Unlike a will, a trust can avoid probate, the court-supervised process for transferring assets after death.

Durable powers of attorney in South Carolina

A durable power of attorney lets a person you choose (your agent) handle financial matters when you can’t, or don’t want to: paying bills, managing investments, and applying for government benefits such as Medicaid.

Worth knowing: in South Carolina, adult children, and even spouses, cannot sign a Medicaid application for an aging loved one unless they have been appointed under that person’s power of attorney.

South Carolina’s rules for powers of attorney and health care directives are very specific, and documents must follow them to be valid. If your documents were signed in another state, banks and hospitals here may not be obligated to accept them, and families often don’t find out until the moment they need to use them, when it may be too late to sign new ones.

A power of attorney ends if a competent principal revokes it, by court order, or at the principal’s death, and it can also be written to end on a specific date.

Ten common estate planning mistakes

From drafting wills, to administering estates, to disputes in court, these are the issues that come up again and again:

  1. Do-it-yourself wills and trustsA will found online and signed without the formalities the law requires may not be admitted to probate at all.
  2. Improper use of, or failure to fund, “lifetime” trustsA trust only works for the assets actually placed in it.
  3. Failing to write a will or revocable trust at allWithout one, property passes by intestacy, to whoever state law says inherits.
  4. Not coordinating beneficiary designations with your willAccounts with named beneficiaries pass outside the will.
  5. Not coordinating retirement accounts with your estate planDesignations affect how and when beneficiaries are taxed.
  6. Outright bequests to young or immature childrenMoney left outright may be spent before a child is ready.
  7. No flexibility in the planLaws and families change; a good plan allows for it.
  8. Trusting people to “do the right thing”In short: don’t “trust.” Establish a trust.
  9. Owning your own life insuranceProceeds can be exposed to estate taxes if you own the policy.
  10. Having a non-trusts-and-estates lawyer prepare your planEstate planning is its own specialty.

Common questions

Can my adult children sign a Medicaid application for me?

Not in South Carolina unless they have been appointed under your power of attorney. The same is true for spouses.

Will my power of attorney from another state work in South Carolina?

It may not. South Carolina’s rules are very specific, and third parties such as banks and hospitals may not be obligated to accept documents from another state.
