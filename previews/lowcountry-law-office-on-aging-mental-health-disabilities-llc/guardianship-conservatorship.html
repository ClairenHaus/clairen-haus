Practice area

Guardianship and conservatorship.

When someone loses mental capacity and is unlikely to regain it, it may be necessary to ask the county Probate Court to appoint a guardian and a conservator.

When it becomes necessary

A loss of capacity can come from dementia (Alzheimer’s, Lewy body and others), cancer, congestive heart failure, brain injury, stroke, ALS, an intellectual or developmental disability such as cerebral palsy, Down syndrome or autism, or another life-altering illness or injury. A guardian and conservator may be needed so your loved one can get medical care, apply for and receive government benefits, have bills paid and finances managed, and be protected from abuse or neglect.

Two different jobs

Guardian

Makes day-to-day living decisions, placement decisions, and health care and medical decisions.

Conservator

Makes financial decisions, pays bills, and applies for and obtains government benefits.

The same person may serve as both, but they are technically two separate appointments. More than one person can serve as co-guardians or co-conservators. A professional guardian or conservator can also be appointed, which is often the better choice when there is no family, or no appropriate family member.

Bonding: to be appointed conservator, a person must be bonded. Someone who cannot obtain a bond will not be appointed. No bond is required to serve as guardian.

Do you need an attorney?

It depends on the county Probate Court where your loved one lives. In Charleston County, an attorney is required for these cases. In Berkeley and Dorchester counties, an attorney is not required, but the Court strongly urges families to have one, especially if the case is likely to be contested.

A case is “contested” when family members disagree about who should be appointed, or when someone believes a caregiver has been abusive or has misused the person’s money or property.

Common questions

What is the difference between a guardian and a conservator?

A guardian makes day-to-day living, placement and medical decisions. A conservator handles finances, pays bills and applies for government benefits. One person can serve as both, but they are separate appointments.

Do I need a lawyer for a guardianship in Charleston County?

Yes. Charleston County requires an attorney for guardianship and conservatorship cases. Berkeley and Dorchester counties do not, but strongly urge families to have one.
