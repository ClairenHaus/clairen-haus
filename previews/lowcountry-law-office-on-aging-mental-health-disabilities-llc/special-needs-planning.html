Practice area

Special needs planning: planning for two generations.

Many adult children with a disability need support and protection their whole lives, long after their parents are gone. We help families build plans that give a loved one a decent quality of life while preserving their government benefits.

The question every parent asks

One of the biggest worries for parents of a child with special needs is what will happen after they are no longer here. Will their child’s quality of life continue? How can you provide for the things they want and need without disqualifying them from government support?

What a special needs trust is

A special needs trust (SNT) holds property for the benefit of a child or adult with a disability. It is designed to supplement, not replace, government benefits such as SSI. It can hold cash, personal property, real estate or life insurance proceeds. Because the trustee has sole discretion over distributions, the trust can pay for things that improve quality of life: medical and dental costs, equipment such as wheelchairs or an accessible van, education and therapy, transportation, personal attendants, recreation, trips and more.

The Social Security Administration has allowed assets to be held in trust for an SSI recipient since 1975, as long as the beneficiary cannot control the distributions or revoke the trust.

Who sets one up, and who manages it

  • Parents or other family members can create an SNT as part of their own estate plan, so a child’s inheritance flows into the trust instead of to the child directly.
  • A person with a disability who expects an inheritance or another large sum can establish one; sometimes a court order is needed.
  • The trustee can be a parent or loved one, a bank, a financial planner, a CPA or a professional fiduciary. Parents usually serve first, with a successor named in advance. We often recommend a corporate co-trustee to help family trustees as laws and tax rules change.
  • The beneficiary cannot serve as trustee, because the premise of the trust is that they don’t control its funds.

When to plan

Earlier is better. Many SNTs are funded with life insurance, which costs less the younger and healthier you are. Without an SNT in place, grandparents, aunts and uncles have no safe way to leave money to your child without putting benefits at risk. Even if your child’s future is unclear, it is never too early to plan for a parent’s sudden death or disability.

A simple will is better than no will, but it is rarely enough on its own. A special needs trust works best when it is coordinated with your other documents and your financial assets.

Common questions

What is a special needs trust?

A trust that holds property for a child or adult with a disability and is designed to supplement, not replace, government benefits such as SSI.

Can the person with a disability be the trustee?

No. The premise of a special needs trust is that the beneficiary does not control the trust’s principal or income.

Can other relatives add money to the trust?

Yes. Anyone can add property at any time, by gift during life, by will or living trust, or through life insurance, employee plan or retirement plan benefits.

Is a special needs trust private?

Yes. If the person creating it wishes, the trust can remain confidential.
