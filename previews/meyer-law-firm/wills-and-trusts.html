Wills and Trusts

What happens to your belongings at death depends on what kind of plan you have, or do not have. Wills and trusts are the foundation of most estate plans.

Which one best meets your needs depends on many factors: your marital status and family situation, whether there are minor children or children with special needs, your finances, and the tax implications of your assets.

Wills

A will puts in writing how you want your assets distributed at death. Without one, the State of Florida directs how your assets are distributed. A will lets you name your beneficiaries, decide when they receive their shares, and even provide for generations beyond, such as great-grandchildren.

Your will also names who will administer your estate, who will administer any trusts created under your will, and who you trust to raise your minor children. Meyer Law Firm works with you to define your wishes and goals, then designs a will you are comfortable with and makes sure it is in accordance with the law.

  • Advantages: retitling assets is not necessary, creditors have only a limited time to make claims, and you can appoint guardians for minor children.
  • Disadvantages: a will must go through probate, can become outdated after major life changes, and is effective only at death, so it cannot address incapacity. In these cases a trust may better address your concerns.

Trusts

Trusts that would benefit your family vary in type and complexity depending on your personal and financial situation. For instance, Meyer Law Firm may recommend a Revocable Living Trust, which lets you name yourself as both trustee and beneficiary during your lifetime. Because select assets are transferred to the trust before death, your family avoids probate expenses on them. More importantly, the trust provides for your financial care if you are alive but not well.

Common questions about wills and trusts

Answers drawn from the questions new clients ask the firm most often. They are general information, not legal advice for your situation.

What can my will do?

A will can:

  • Name who receives your property at death.
  • Specify when individuals will receive their shares of the estate.
  • Provide distributions to individuals who are generations beyond such as great-grandchildren.
  • Designate the person you want to administer your estate. This may be an individual or a bank or trust company.
  • Include a trust which distributes the income from a portion of your estate for the benefit of family or others. This is often used to care for minors.
  • Name a person who will administer any trusts created under your will.
  • Allow real estate and other assets to be sold without court proceedings.
  • Allow you to make charitable gifts.
  • Determine who bears any tax burden.
  • Name a trusted person to care for your minor or special needs children (Guardian).
Are there any disadvantages to a will?

Yes. The key disadvantages to a will are:

  • Since a will is only effective at death, there is no opportunity to plan for incapacity.
  • A will must go through probate, a proceeding that may involve time and expense.
  • A will can become outdated if you have major changes in your life, such as marriage, the birth of a child or a death in the family.
What happens if I die without a will?

If you die without a will, known as dying “intestate”, Florida law decides how your assets are divided. That law is intended to distribute assets the way an average person might have, which may not be at all what you want. Even if you have told your loved ones your wishes, they likely will not be able to honor them unless they are in a legal document.

After probate payment of claims, debts, taxes and expenses, your assets are divided between your spouse and children depending on your family situation. A few scenarios:

  • Married without children: your spouse receives all of your intestate estate.
  • Married with children, all of whom are also your spouse’s, and your spouse has no other descendants: your spouse receives all of your intestate estate.
  • Married with children, and your spouse also has children from another marriage: your spouse receives half, and the balance is divided equally among your own children.
  • Not married, no children: your estate goes to your parents, or to your siblings if your parents have passed.
Can a will reduce expenses and taxes?

Yes. A will frequently will reduce expenses. If you do not have a will, the court will appoint a person, known or unknown to you, to manage your estate. In these cases, the cost of probate may be higher and the administration of your estate may be subject to greater court supervision.

A well thought-out estate plan can also incorporate important income tax advantages.

Can I distribute all of my property?

There are select property interests that cannot be transferred by your will. For example, some property rights automatically terminate upon death, and property jointly owned may transfer automatically to survivors.

Must I leave money to my spouse and children?

In Florida, you may not disinherit your spouse without a legal marital agreement.

If you get married after your will and do not have a marital agreement, your new spouse may receive the same share of your estate as if you had died without a will.

You can specifically exclude children in your will.

How long will my will last?

When properly executed, your will is valid until you decide to make changes to it. You can update your will as often as you like to meet changes in your life.

I just moved to Florida. Do I need to redo my will?

It is recommended that you have your will reviewed by a Florida attorney to make sure it is properly drafted and executed according to the state’s laws.

Is a trust a substitute for a will?

Typically a trust is used in conjunction with a will. A trust specifies how property put into it is handled and can also address tax consequences. Learn more on the Estate Planning page.

What is a Living Will?

A living will should not be confused with a will. A living will is only used if you are not able to make your own health care decisions, and it states your wishes as to life-sustaining procedures.
