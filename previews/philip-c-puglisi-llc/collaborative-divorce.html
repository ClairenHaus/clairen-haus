Collaborative Divorce in New Jersey

In a collaborative divorce, both spouses have attorneys at their side, and everyone signs on to settling out of court. Philip Puglisi practices collaborative law in Bergen, Passaic and Essex counties.

An alternative to a litigated divorce

The collaborative divorce process is an alternative to a contested divorce that proceeds through the court system. It has been around for years, but only relatively recently has it been widely recognized and used.

The advantages

  • It is less expensive than litigation, because both spouses commit in writing to settling their issues.
  • It creates an amicable atmosphere that is conducive to settlement.
  • The spouses control the entire process. The court is not involved until a comprehensive Marital Settlement Agreement has been reached.
  • It establishes a level playing field, with much less emotional stress, fear and anxiety.
  • Children are a priority: the spouses commit to a resolution that serves their children.

The participation agreement

Each spouse is represented by counsel, and at the outset everyone signs a “Participation Agreement”, a promise to move affirmatively and amicably toward a resolution. Everything is transparent and laid out on the table. Instead of one spouse having to go to court to compel the other to produce financial documents, information is exchanged freely and voluntarily, which avoids an expensive and time-consuming step. The same is true of support while the case is pending, which in litigation often requires a separate application to the court.

Help from other professionals

The collaborative process can bring in mental health professionals trained in the process, which is especially helpful when custody and parenting time are at issue. They may sit in on meetings between the spouses and counsel, offer proposals and help the overall resolution.

Collaborative divorce or mediation?

In a collaborative divorce, both attorneys are involved from the start. In mediation, the spouses essentially negotiate on their own, guided by a trained mediator who cannot give legal advice or advocate for either side. If you would be more comfortable with an attorney beside you throughout, the collaborative route may suit you better. If you only need to consult an attorney from time to time, mediation might work for you. Both depend on a commitment to resolve the issues, and when bitterness or one spouse’s refusal to act reasonably makes that impossible, litigation is the path left.
