Child Custody & Parenting Time Lawyer in Bergen County

New Jersey gives both parents equal rights to the care and upbringing of their children. Philip Puglisi helps parents build custody and parenting time arrangements that protect the children and can actually work.

Both parents have equal rights

New Jersey’s custody statutes are designed to give both parents regular, consistent and ongoing contact with their children after a divorce. Along with that right comes the obligation to provide financial, moral and other support.

Legal custody and physical custody

Legal custody is decision making on the major questions affecting a child’s health, education and welfare. Most of the time parents share joint legal custody, so each has a say.

Physical or residential custody is about where the child actually lives. Under a traditional parenting plan, the child lives with one parent most of the time, the “parent of primary residence”, and the “parent of alternate residence” typically has liberal parenting time. Parents can make any arrangement they see fit, so long as the children’s interests are protected, including a fully equal, shared arrangement. That works best when the parents are amicable and live close to each other. In some cases, parenting time must be supervised, at least for a period, where one parent’s behavior has created or will create a risk of harm to the child.

What a custody and parenting time agreement should cover

  • Who is the primary residential parent
  • Joint legal custody and decision making
  • Access to school events, teachers and counselors
  • The weekly parenting time schedule
  • Holidays, summer vacation and school breaks
  • Pickups, drop-offs and driving to activities
  • Phone calls and other contact with the children
  • Relocation limits, including any radius restriction
  • Children’s and parents’ birthdays
  • Introducing a significant other to the children

Moving out of New Jersey with the children

New Jersey law prohibits a custodial parent from relocating out of state with the children without the other parent’s written consent or a court order. The court can allow a move, and the guiding star is the best interest of the children. In Bisbing v. Bisbing, the New Jersey Supreme Court required a full best-interest evaluation, rather than only a good-faith reason for the move. Where parents share a truly equal arrangement, relocation is harder, because the request is really a request to change custody.
