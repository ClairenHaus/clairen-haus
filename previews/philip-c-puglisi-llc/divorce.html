Three ways to get divorced in New Jersey

At your first consultation, Philip walks you through the three avenues to a divorce, whether your case belongs in Bergen County, Passaic County or another county in North Jersey: the traditional litigated divorce, filed in the Superior Court of New Jersey, Chancery Division, Family Part; divorce mediation; and the collaborative divorce process. He handles divorces through any of the three.

Grounds: no-fault and fault

There are essentially two types of filings. Most New Jersey divorces are no-fault divorces based on “irreconcilable differences”: differences that have caused the marriage to break down with no prospect of reconciliation. The advantage is that the “dirty laundry” between the spouses stays out of the case.

A fault divorce rests on grounds such as adultery, abandonment, incarceration or severe substance abuse. A common misconception is that the court will award the non-faulting spouse more in assets or alimony. In most circumstances that is not the case, and a fault filing is usually more expensive, because the spouse claiming fault carries the burden of proof.

Uncontested divorce

A divorce is “uncontested” when the spouses have reached agreement on every issue arising from the marriage: alimony and child support, property and businesses, debt, life and health insurance, taxes and college for the children. Not only can a divorce proceed this way, it is the preferred route, and settled matters are scheduled ahead of contested ones. An uncontested divorce is different from a no-fault divorce: the issues may have been resolved through mediation, the collaborative process, or a litigated case that settled before trial.

Separating without divorcing

New Jersey does not offer a “legal separation”, and the court will not sign an order declaring spouses separated. You are free to separate physically for as long as you choose. If you do, it is important to live under a written agreement covering finances, custody and parenting time, and to get advice on your rights before you sign one.

How the litigated process works

  • The complaint. One spouse files a complaint for divorce in the county where either spouse lives. Once it is filed, Philip’s office has it served on your spouse through a licensed New Jersey process server, or on your spouse’s attorney.
  • The answer. The other spouse has 35 days from service to respond, usually with an answer and a counterclaim.
  • Case Management Conference. The judge learns whether custody, a business valuation or other major issues are in play, asks whether experts will be retained, and sets a period for discovery.
  • Early Settlement Panel. Three volunteer lawyers at the courthouse recommend how the case should settle. The recommendations are non-binding. Philip has sat on the Bergen County Early Settlement Panel for more than 20 years.
  • Economic mediation. If the panel does not settle the case, the court sends the parties to mandatory economic mediation with a mediator chosen from the county roster.
  • Trial. Only if mediation fails does the judge set the matter down for trial.

How long does it take?

It depends on how complex the issues are. If most issues are resolved or can be resolved quickly, a divorce can conceivably be completed in a matter of months. Some counties, including Bergen County, require a case to be completed, by settlement or trial, within one year of the filing of the complaint. Once the complaint is filed, the court controls the scheduling.
