Modifying Alimony & Child Support After Divorce in NJ

Life changes after a divorce. When it changes significantly, alimony and child support can change too. Philip Puglisi also enforces marital agreements and court orders when the other side does not comply.

A significant change in circumstances

Under New Jersey law, alimony can be modified after a significant change in circumstances affecting either party. If the supported spouse becomes disabled or legitimately cannot find full-time work, he or she may be able to seek an increase. It works in reverse as well: if the paying spouse’s earnings or ability to earn are legitimately reduced, he or she may be able to seek a reduction. How the Marital Settlement Agreement was structured matters, and the same principles apply to changes affecting child support.

Cohabitation

Alimony may also be modified or ended if the supported spouse is cohabiting with another person. Whether cohabitation exists is a very fact-sensitive question. It means a relationship akin to marriage, more than dating or sharing a residence. Courts look at whether the couple holds itself out as married, lives together, shares finances and living expenses and household chores, and whether one subsidizes the other.

One of the seminal New Jersey Supreme Court cases on cohabitation is Gayet v. Gayet (1983), which Philip had the opportunity to work on as a young attorney. Since then, courts have balanced a former spouse’s right to a personal relationship against the fairness of continuing alimony. Where possible, it is wise to address cohabitation in the settlement agreement itself.

Post-divorce enforcement

When a former spouse stops paying support or does not comply with the marital settlement agreement, the court can be asked to enforce it. For child support, remedies include contempt, sanctions, seizing tax refunds or other benefits, and suspending a driver’s license after a hearing.
