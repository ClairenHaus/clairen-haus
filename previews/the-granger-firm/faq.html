Buying and selling a home

Does someone selling a house “as-is” still have to fill out a seller’s disclosure form in Pennsylvania?

In general, yes. Even when the parties agree to an as-is deal, Pennsylvania’s Seller Disclosure Law still requires disclosure of known material defects, unless an exemption applies. A “material defect” is a problem that would have a significant adverse impact on the property’s value or that involves an unreasonable risk to people on the property. The seller must give the buyer a signed disclosure form before the agreement of sale is signed for buildings of one to four units. Exemptions include transfers by an executor administering an estate and sales of new construction with a warranty and certificate of occupancy. A seller is not liable for an inaccuracy the seller did not know of, or reasonably believed had been corrected. The law does not apply to commercial properties.

If a buyer changes his or her mind just before settlement, can the seller force the buyer to complete the purchase?

Pennsylvania recognizes suits for specific performance against a defaulting buyer, but they are limited to very specific situations under current case law. More common are suits for damages arising from the default (such as a lower price on resale) or to retain the buyer’s deposit. The time and cost of litigation, and the buyer’s ability to pay a judgment, must all be weighed, and buyers often claim a different reason for not proceeding.

If a buyer could not get a mortgage because the buyer’s own house did not sell, does the buyer get the deposit back?

Under the Pennsylvania Association of Realtors form agreement of sale, the most commonly used residential form, a buyer who fails to obtain a mortgage because of the failure to sell the buyer’s residence is entitled to a refund of the deposit, though whether the buyer met all of its obligations may become an issue. The recent PAR form also lets the seller terminate if the mortgage commitment is conditioned on a sale the agreement did not provide for.

Should buyers purchase title insurance?

Generally, yes, and a lender will likely require it. Subject to its conditions and exclusions, title insurance protects against defects in the chain of title, such as forgeries, improper recording or indexing, unpaid taxes, unmarketable title and lack of access. Unlike most insurance it covers the past, for a one-time fee, and lasts as long as the buyer owns the property.

How much does title insurance cost?

Relatively little compared with the cost of the real estate. Pennsylvania title insurers file their rates with the Department of Insurance, usually through the Title Insurance Rating Bureau of Pennsylvania (TIRBOP), which publishes its members’ rates. The cost depends mainly on the amount of coverage, which is usually tied to the purchase price or loan amount, plus any endorsements.

How long does a party have to file a lawsuit?

It depends on the claim. A suit against a home inspector must be brought within one year of the date the inspection report is given to the buyer. A claim under the Seller Disclosure Law must be filed within two years of closing. Fraud and negligence generally carry two years, but whether time has run can depend on other factors, such as when the person knew or should have known of the problem. The applicable period should always be reviewed by counsel.

Property and land

What is adverse possession?

A legal theory that permits a claimant to obtain title to land through actual, continuous, exclusive, visible, notorious, distinct and hostile possession for twenty-one years. In City of Philadelphia v. Galdo (Pa. 2019), a case handled by David J. Scaggs, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that a city can be subject to such a claim once it has abandoned the land’s public use.

What should I know before buying a house with stucco?

Have a stucco inspection by a qualified inspector. Because you cannot see behind stucco, you will likely need the seller’s permission to probe it. Pennsylvania law requires sellers to disclose known stucco problems, and even a remediated home may need a well-documented repair report to sell.

Who pays realty transfer tax in Pennsylvania?

Recording the deed triggers the tax, which is on the property rather than the person; buyer and seller are each technically responsible for seeing it paid. The Commonwealth charges 1%, counties typically another 1%, some municipalities more, and Philadelphia charges 3% instead of 1%. The parties can agree who pays, but that agreement does not bind the taxing authorities.
