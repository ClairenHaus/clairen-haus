FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Plain answers to the estate planning questions Lynn hears most often.

Why should I have a Will?

A Will lets you state clearly who you would like to receive your assets when you die. It is also the document in which you name guardians for minor children (under age 18) and the person who will administer your Will through the probate court, known as the executor or executrix.

What is a health care proxy?

A health care proxy is a legal document that lets you name a person with authority to make health care decisions for you if you are unable to make them yourself.

What is a durable power of attorney?

A durable power of attorney is a legal document that lets you name a person to have legal authority over all of your financial affairs if you become incapacitated.

Why would I want to give someone authority over my assets?

Single and married people very often have assets in their name alone, or assets that require their own signature. If the owner became incapacitated, no one else could reach those assets. A durable power of attorney is a cost-effective way for a family member or friend to take charge of financial affairs in the event of incapacity.

What if I die without a Will?

Dying “intestate” means Massachusetts law, not your wishes, decides who inherits your assets, and the court selects executors and guardians for your minor children. There is no way to make specific gifts or do estate tax planning, and probating your estate will take longer and cost more.

Does a Will control where all of my assets go?

No. Your Will only controls assets in your name alone at the time of death. Jointly owned assets (such as a home owned jointly by a married couple, or a bank account owned jointly by a parent and child) do not pass through your Will, and neither do assets with a named beneficiary, such as life insurance, IRAs and retirement plans.

Does a Will avoid probate?

No, exactly the opposite. A Will makes sure your affairs are administered through the probate court, which is often costly and slow. With proper planning, the involvement of the probate court can be minimized or avoided completely.

Can I change my Will?

Yes, but changing a Will requires signing an amendment, called a codicil, with the same witness and notary requirements as signing the original Will.

What is a living trust?

A trust is a written document under which a trustee holds legal title to property for a beneficiary. A living (“inter vivos”) trust is one you create while you’re alive. You can be the trustee of your own living trust, keeping full control of the trust property during your lifetime, and you can change or revoke it.

Why would I have a living trust?

Different kinds of living trusts can help you avoid probate, reduce estate taxes and preserve wealth for your heirs, give guardians the funds to care for minor children, and direct distributions to children or other beneficiaries beyond age eighteen.

Does my trust go through probate?

A living trust is designed to avoid the probate court. If properly drafted, the trust and the assets it controls at the creator’s death will be free from probate court involvement.

Does a trust have the same witness formalities as a Will?

No. Under Massachusetts law, a trust is valid if signed by the creator and by the trustee. Because a Will’s witness formalities don’t apply, a trust is easier to change from time to time.

These answers are general information about Massachusetts law, not legal advice for your situation. See also 10 myths about estate planning.
