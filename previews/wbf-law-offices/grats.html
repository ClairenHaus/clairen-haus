GRATs: passing on the growth, not just the asset.

Passing future growth in an asset to family at a reduced gift tax cost.

What a GRAT is

A grantor retained annuity trust is an irrevocable trust you fund with an asset while keeping the right to receive fixed annual payments (the annuity) for a set number of years. When the term ends, whatever is left in the trust passes to the beneficiaries you named, often your children or a trust for them.

Why it can reduce gift tax

The taxable gift is measured when the GRAT is created: it is the value of the asset minus the present value of the annuity you keep, calculated with an interest rate the IRS publishes each month. If the asset grows faster than that rate, the excess growth passes to the beneficiaries at the end of the term without further gift tax. A GRAT can be designed so the taxable gift at the start is very small.

Where GRATs are used

  • Assets expected to appreciate, such as closely held business interests or concentrated stock positions.
  • Situations where you want to keep an income stream for a time while shifting future growth to the next generation.

Things to weigh

If you die during the annuity term, much or all of the trust’s value is generally included in your estate, so the term length matters. If the asset doesn’t outperform the IRS rate, the property simply comes back to you through the annuity payments, and little is lost beyond the cost of setting it up. GRATs are grantor trusts, so the trust’s income is taxed to you during the term.

This page explains the topic in general terms. It is not legal advice, and the right approach depends on your circumstances and on the law at the time you plan.
